# Shiro Kuramata

*1934–1991. The Japanese designer who spent thirty years trying to make furniture weigh nothing.*

![The signature piece. How High the Moon, 1986. Nickel-plated expanded steel mesh. Produced by Vitra.](shiro-kuramata-images/s_hhtm.jpg)

*The signature piece. How High the Moon, 1986. Nickel-plated expanded steel mesh. Produced by Vitra.*

![The designer. Shiro Kuramata.](shiro-kuramata-images/jp_portrait.jpg)

*The designer. Shiro Kuramata.*

Shiro Kuramata is the rare designer whose best pieces are instantly recognisable and still hard to explain. A chair made of six sheets of glass and nothing else. An armchair of nickel-plated steel mesh that reads as a drawing of a chair rather than a chair. A block of clear acrylic with red paper roses frozen inside it.

He worked in Tokyo for his entire career, built most of his reputation through interiors that were torn down within a few years, and was dead at 56. Yet he is one of a handful of Japanese designers with a secure place in the Western canon, collected by MoMA, the Met and the Vitra Design Museum, and the subject of a 600-piece Phaidon monograph.

This report is a primer: who he was, what he was trying to do, the pieces you should know with pictures of each, and the lenses from his work that are worth stealing. Sources and image credits are at the end.


## Why he matters

- **He treated materials as the idea.** Acrylic, glass, expanded steel mesh and terrazzo were not finishes applied to a form. Each piece asks what a chair or a cabinet becomes when it is made from one industrial material and nothing else.
- **He pursued weightlessness and transparency as a lifelong theme.** Gravity was something to escape. His pieces try to float, dissolve, or exist as outlines.
- **He bridged Japanese minimalism and Italian postmodernism.** A founding member of Memphis in 1981, he brought restraint to a movement known for noise, and brought colour and play back to Tokyo.
- **He designed from memory and dreams, not from function.** He kept dream journals, named chairs after jazz standards and film characters, and said openly that he was interested in furniture that is not meant to be used.
- **His influence is quiet but everywhere.** Cantilevered shelves with folded clothes in a fashion boutique, cast resin with objects suspended inside, mesh furniture, and the idea that a shop interior can be an artwork all trace back to him.

## Life in brief

![1979. Kuramata in 1979. Photo by Kazumi Kurigami.](shiro-kuramata-images/d_portrait1979.jpg)

*1979. Kuramata in 1979. Photo by Kazumi Kurigami.*

Born in Tokyo in 1934, Kuramata grew up through the war and the occupation. The shortage years left him with a lasting fascination for new, abundant industrial materials. He found design almost by accident, working at the Teikoku Kizai furniture factory in 1954, then trained at the Kuwasawa Design Institute, the most progressive design school in Japan at the time.

His first decade was spent in the display departments of Tokyo department stores, learning to make retail spaces feel like something more than shops. He opened his own office in 1965 and never left Tokyo, though by the late 1980s he had a second studio in Paris and clients on three continents.

- **1934** Born 29 November in Tokyo.
- **1953** Finishes a woodworking course at Tokyo Metropolitan Polytechnic High School.
- **1954–56** Teikoku Kizai furniture factory; then interior design at the Kuwasawa Design Institute.
- **1957–63** Display and interior departments of Tokyo department stores, including San-Ai in Ginza.
- **1965** Opens Kuramata Design Office in Tokyo. Begins the acrylic experiments that define his early work.
- **1970** *Furniture in Irregular Forms, Side 1 and Side 2*: the wavy plywood chests that first bring him international attention.
- **1972** *Oba-Q* lamps. Mainichi Industrial Design Award.
- **1976** *Glass Chair*. First Issey Miyake boutique, in Minami-Aoyama; the collaboration runs for the rest of his life.
- **1981** Invited by Ettore Sottsass to be a founding member of Memphis in Milan. Receives the Japan Cultural Design Prize.
- **1986** *How High the Moon*, later put into production by Vitra.
- **1988** *Miss Blanche*. Opens a Paris studio. Kiyotomo sushi bar, Tokyo.
- **1990** Made a Chevalier of the Ordre des Arts et des Lettres by the French Ministry of Culture.
- **1991** Dies 1 February in Tokyo of heart failure caused by myocarditis, aged 56.

## The ideas

![Dream journal. A page from the sketchbook Words, Dreams, Memories, 1980s.](shiro-kuramata-images/w_sketch.jpg)

*Dream journal. A page from the sketchbook Words, Dreams, Memories, 1980s.*

**Weightlessness.** Kuramata said he wanted to be free of gravity, and almost every mature piece is an attempt at it. The *Glass Chair* has no visible structure. *How High the Moon* keeps the silhouette of a plump club chair but replaces all its mass with a mesh you can see through. *Miss Blanche* holds flowers mid-air. He also hated seams, reportedly calling them the hardest part of any object because they reveal that the thing is finite.

**Memory and dreams.** He kept dream journals and sketchbooks, one titled *Words, Dreams, Memories*. Childhood smells, the sound of a record, a line from a play were his raw material.

**Transparency and immateriality.** Acrylic in the 1960s and 70s, glass in the mid-70s, expanded metal from the mid-80s. Each is a material that lets light and space pass through. The goal was never a clear chair for its own sake; it was an object that reads as an outline of itself, an apparition in the room rather than a presence.

**Names as mood.** Many works are named after jazz standards (*How High the Moon*, *Sing Sing Sing*, *Begin the Beguine*, *Apple Honey*, *Twilight Time*) or after records themselves (*Side 1*, *Side 2*). *Miss Blanche* is named for Blanche DuBois in *A Streetcar Named Desire*. *Oba-Q* is a cartoon ghost.

**Function as a side effect.** Everything he made works. The 49-drawer cabinet opens, the glass chair holds roughly 100 kilograms, the seven-handed clock tells time. But function was not the starting point. He described furniture that simply happens to manifest as furniture, and most of his drawer pieces were made without a client.

> I am interested in furniture that is not intended to be used, but simply happens to manifest as furniture.
>
> Shiro Kuramata, quoted in the 2023 Setagaya Art Museum retrospective

> Memories, including the dream experiences I had, for me, constitute an infinite cosmos.
>
> Shiro Kuramata, from his notebooks

> Blanche DuBois herself is fake.
>
> Kuramata, on why the roses in Miss Blanche had to be artificial

## Interiors: the work that disappeared

Kuramata designed over 350 interiors: bars, restaurants, clubs and shops in Tokyo and later abroad. Almost none survive. He knew they were temporary and designed them that way. Three stand out.

![Kiyotomo, interior. Inside. Cedar panels with gaps accurate to three millimetres.](shiro-kuramata-images/dz_kiyotomo_b.jpg)

*Kiyotomo, interior. Inside. Cedar panels with gaps accurate to three millimetres.*

- **Kiyotomo sushi bar (1988).** Opened by a young chef, closed in the early 1990s after the bubble burst, and left vacant for years. M+ acquired it in 2014, with craftsmen from Ishimaru, his long-time fabricator, dismantling and reinstalling it. Conservators found three layers of blue paint at the entrance, suggesting Kuramata changed his mind on site.
- **Issey Miyake boutiques (1976 onward).** He designed dozens of Miyake shops, from Minami-Aoyama in Tokyo to Bergdorf Goodman in New York and Paris. His innovation was to lay garments flat on thin cantilevered shelves rather than hang them, so the clothes read as objects and the shop as a gallery.
- **Oblomov bar, Il Palazzo, Fukuoka (1989).** Commissioned by Aldo Rossi for his hotel. Kuramata made a monumental floor lamp for it with synthetic roses cast into the acrylic shaft, a cousin of Miss Blanche.

## Memphis and Sottsass

![Revolving, red. Revolving Cabinet in Cappellini's current production.](shiro-kuramata-images/m_rev_b.jpg)

*Revolving, red. Revolving Cabinet in Cappellini's current production.*

Kuramata met Ettore Sottsass in the 1970s and the two became close. When Sottsass launched Memphis in Milan in 1981 he invited Kuramata as a founding member, the only Japanese designer in the group. Kuramata's Memphis pieces (*Imperial*, *Kyoto*, *Sally*) share the movement's appetite for colour and surprising material but keep his stillness.

The exchange ran both ways. Memphis gave him an international stage and the permission to be playful; his restraint gave Memphis one of its few quiet voices. He later called Sottsass his maestro. The 21_21 Design Sight exhibition in Tokyo in 2010 was devoted to the pair.

## Lenses worth stealing

- **Pick one material and push it to its limit.** The Glass Chair is only glass. Mesh chairs are only mesh. The constraint is the concept.
- **Keep the familiar silhouette, remove the mass.** *How High the Moon* works because it is recognisably an armchair. The surprise is in what is missing, not in a new shape.
- **Hide the seams.** A joint that disappears changes how an object feels. Invest in the adhesive, the weld, the tolerance.
- **Name things after feelings.** A jazz standard or a film character gives an object a mood before anyone touches it.
- **Let display be the design.** The Miyake shelves prove a store fixture can define a brand more than the architecture around it.
- **Suspend something fragile in something permanent.** Roses in resin, feathers in acrylic. Contrast of the ephemeral and the fixed is a reliable source of emotion.
- **Design from a dream journal.** Keep one. His best ideas came from memory, not from a brief.
